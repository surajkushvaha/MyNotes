# /isometric: a tiny isometric world that builds itself

Example subject: "a tiny ramen bar". The user may give any small place or object: a bookshop, a lighthouse, a bakery, a campsite.

## The skill: /isometric

### Look
- Ground: a vertical gradient, dusk before the lights, cool and dark (top `#1c3a44`, bottom `#0e1d24`). When the lights come on it shifts to a warmer sage to teal (top `#4f7f86`, bottom `#2a4a52`).
- A small slab floats in the middle of the canvas (about 760 px wide), seen in true isometric projection: a tile is 2 units wide and 1 unit tall on screen. Use `iso(x, y, z) = [cx + (x - y) * 44, cy + (x + y) * 22 - z * 44]`.
- Everything is made of boxes and simple shapes with flat colour. Every box has three faces: top (lightest), left (mid), right (darkest), taken from one colour at +12 percent, 0 and -22 percent lightness. Soft contact shadows (a blurred dark ellipse) under every piece and a big soft shadow under the slab.
- Palette: 6 to 8 colours that suit the subject. Warm woods, one strong accent (the sign, the awnings), warm yellow light colour `#ffcf6e`. Pick them for the subject: a ramen bar gets dark wood, red lanterns, cream noren curtains.
- Type: top left mono label `ISOMETRIC` (small, 26 px, letter-spaced, 60 percent white), under it the subject name in weight 800 (about 96 px, white). Top right `PIECES` in the same small mono with the count under it in orange `#ff9a4a` (weight 800, 96 px).

### What to build
Invent about 40 pieces for the subject, listed in a build order array. Each piece has a `draw(ctx)` function, a position, and a landing time. The order goes from the bottom up: floor planks, back wall, side wall, shelves, main counter or furniture, stools or seats, the main prop (pot, desk, oven), small props (bowls, jars, books), roof in rows, lantern strings or lamps, signs and boards, a plant, and last a tiny character (cook, keeper, baker) who hops in. Keep every piece inside the slab and make sure no sign or board is cut off by the roof or the canvas edge.

### Beats (seconds)
- 0.0: the empty slab (floor base only) with its shadow, the two labels and `PIECES 0` already on screen. The ground is in its dusk colours.
- 0.1 to 3.2: pieces drop in one after another from 140 px above. Each piece falls for 0.33 s with `easeInCubic`, lands with a squash (scaleY 0.88, then `easeOutBack` back to 1 over 0.27 s), and its contact shadow grows while it falls. Small pieces release a tiny dust puff (3 to 5 grey dots that spread and fade in 0.27 s). Stagger 0.07 to 0.1 s between pieces, overlapping groups, so all 40 land by 3.2 s. `PIECES` ticks up by one on every landing and punches on the last one.
- 3.3 to 3.75, the hero moment, lights on: the warm light spill fades in on the floor (0.27 s), the lanterns or lamps ignite one by one 0.1 s apart (glow grows with `easeOutBack`), windows and signs glow, and the ground colour shifts to the warm sage-teal.
- 3.6 to 5.0: idle. Steam rises from anything hot (soft wisps on a seeded sine path), hanging things sway 2 degrees out of phase, the character bobs and moves one arm, the slab floats 4 px on a slow sine, the light flickers by 3 percent.

- Every main move drags a small secondary move with it (a shadow, a bounce, a glow, dust).
- Nothing is ever fully still: after the main event the picture keeps living at low level (a slow 2 to 4 percent push, a breathing glow, drifting particles, a 1 to 3 degree sway).

# /globe: every flight leaving one city, on a dotted globe

Example subject: "every flight leaving Tokyo". The user may give any hub: a city, an airport, a port, a company HQ and its offices.

## The skill: /globe

### Look
- Ground: near black navy `#05080f` with a faint radial glow behind the globe.
- A globe about 900 px wide, centered slightly low, drawn with an orthographic projection of latitude and longitude. The globe is made of dots, not a texture: a grid of dots every 2.5 degrees of latitude and longitude, drawn only where there is land, only on the visible hemisphere, smaller toward the edge (radius = 2.6 x the cosine of the angle to the viewer, minimum 0.6 px). Land dots are cool teal `#3fb6c4` at 80 percent; ocean is the dark body of the sphere (`#0b1626`) with a soft rim light and a thin atmosphere glow (`#47c6ff`, 25 percent) around the edge.
- You have no map data and no network. Write 10 to 14 simplified continent and large island polygons yourself, as arrays of `[lat, lon]` points (15 to 40 points each, from your own knowledge of the coastlines, rough is fine), and mark a dot as land when a point-in-polygon test says so. Make sure the hub's region and every destination sit on or next to land.
- Arcs: great-circle arcs lifted off the surface (height proportional to the distance, peak about 18 percent of the globe radius), drawn in warm white `#ffe9c9` with a soft glow (draw the line twice, a wide faint one under a thin bright one) and a bright comet head at the drawing tip. Arcs and city dots are computed on the sphere, so they turn with the globe.
- Type: top left a mono label `HUB / DEPARTURES` (for example `TOKYO / DEPARTURES`), under it a mono line with a clock `09:14:22 JST` (local time zone of the hub) and `034 DEPARTED`. City tags are small mono labels with the 3 letter airport or city code next to each destination dot.

### What to show
The subject gives the hub; pick 7 destinations spread around the world so the arcs fan out in different directions and the globe is turned (start longitude) so the hub is near the middle right and most arcs are visible. Give each destination a 3 letter code.

### Beats (seconds)
- 0.0: the dotted globe is already on screen, no arcs yet, rotating slowly eastward from `t` (about 8 degrees over the whole clip). The title lines are on screen. The hub dot pulses: a ring grows and fades every 0.67 s all clip long.
- 0.27 to 3.0: the 7 arcs launch one by one, about 0.4 s apart. Each arc draws from the hub to its city over 0.6 s (`easeInOutCubic` along its length) with the comet head. On arrival the city dot flashes, one ring pulses out, and its tag pops in (scale 0.7 to 1 with `easeOutBack`). `DEPARTED` ticks up by one on each arrival.
- The clock in the mono line advances one second every 1.0 s of the clip.
- 3.05 to 3.5, the hero moment: the last arc lands, all arcs brighten together for 0.27 s, and the atmosphere glow swells once.
- 3.5 to 5.0: idle. Small light pulses travel along every arc from the hub outward in a loop (seeded offsets), the globe keeps turning, the atmosphere glow breathes.

# /scale: a true-scale pull back that makes a big number land

Example subject: "Earths inside Jupiter" (result: 1,300 Earths fit inside Jupiter). The user may give any pair: coins in a bathtub, Russia vs Pluto, a blue whale vs a bus.

## The skill: /scale

### Look
- Ground: deep space `#030409` with 150 to 200 stars (seeded), tiny white and blue-white dots of 1 to 2.5 px at different brightness, in two or three depth layers.
- Two bodies. The small one (the unit, example: Earth) is drawn with a radial-gradient sphere: detailed colour, lit from the upper left with a soft terminator on the shadow side, a thin atmosphere rim, and a slow rotation or cloud drift computed from `t`. The big one (example: Jupiter) is drawn as a huge circle that is only partly visible at the bottom of the canvas, filled with horizontal bands (draw 14 to 20 wavy bands with varied warm tones, drifting sideways slowly) and, if the subject has one, a signature mark (the Great Red Spot).
- If the pair is not planets, use the same idea with flat, clean, well-lit objects: the small unit first, tight on screen, then the camera pulls back until the big one fills the lower half.
- Type: small caps mono `TRUE SCALE` (28 px, letter-spaced, 60 percent white), then the big number line in weight 800 at about 150 px, white, with the unit word smaller on the same line (`1,300 Earths`), then `fit inside Jupiter` in weight 600, 54 px, 70 percent white. Find the true ratio from your own knowledge and use the exact figure (volume ratio: how many of the small thing fit in the big one).
- A thin ring marker around the small body with a leader line and the label `Earth, to scale` (mono, 26 px) appears at the end.

### Beats (seconds)
- 0.0: tight on the small body: it is large (about 520 px wide) and centered, detailed and rotating slowly. A small label with its name. Stars around. No big body visible yet, no title.
- 0.0 to 3.3: a smooth pull back (`easeInOutCubic`): the small body shrinks to its final size and spot (about 60 px, upper area), star layers move at two speeds (parallax), and the big body's edge rises into view from the bottom and keeps growing until it fills the lower half of the canvas. Its bands drift sideways the whole time.
- 1.3 to 2.0: `TRUE SCALE` fades in and slides up 8 px, then the big number line appears.
- 2.0 to 3.5, the hero moment: the number counts from 1 up to the real figure with `easeOutCubic` (fast, then slow), formatted with thousands commas, and lands at 3.45 s with a punch. `fit inside Jupiter` fades in at 3.2 s.
- 3.3 to 3.9: the thin ring around the small body draws on (stroke from 0 to full), then the leader line and the label draw on and fade in.
- 3.9 to 5.0: idle. The pull back keeps going by 2 percent, bands drift, the atmosphere rim shimmers, a few stars twinkle (seeded).

# /bar-race: a bar chart race that ends on an overtake

Example subject: "most visited cities 2000 to 2026". The user may give any ranking: richest companies, biggest languages, most streamed artists.

## The skill: /bar-race

### Look
- Ground: near black graphite `#0e0f12` with a faint vertical gradient to `#17181c`.
- Type: top left the title in weight 800 (about 84 px, white, for example `Most visited cities`), under it a sub line in weight 600 (34 px, 55 percent white, for example `Visitors per year, 2000 to 2026`). The big year bottom right in weight 800, about 300 px, in `#c9ccd3` at 90 percent, with a gentle drop shadow.
- Eight rows, each 105 px tall with 20 px gap, from about y = 360. Each row: a rounded 3 letter tag chip on the left (34 px mono text in a dark chip), then a bar whose length is the value, then the full name inside the bar start and the value at the end of the bar (`32.4M`, weight 800). Bars are neutral grey `#8a8f99` with a lighter top edge, except the winner, which turns orange `#ff8a3d`.
- A light axis at the top with 3 ticks and grid lines (`10M`, `20M`, `30M`) that slide and rescale as the maximum grows.

### Data
Make up 8 realistic entries and a yearly value series for each from the first year to the last (27 values for 2000 to 2026). Use your own knowledge for the shape of the story: the final year values should be real or very close, the first year order should be different, and one entry must climb from the middle of the pack to first place around 70 to 75 percent of the way through (that is the overtake). Interpolate between the yearly values with a smooth curve (not steps).

### Beats (seconds)
- 0.0: title, sub line, axis and all 8 rows already on screen at the first year's values, in grey. The first year shows big.
- 0.0 to 4.3: the year runs from the first to the last year at an even pace (about 0.16 s per year). The year text changes on whole years with a 6 px upward slide and a fade. Bar widths and value labels interpolate continuously. Rows re-rank by value: a row slides vertically to its new rank (`easeInOutCubic`, 0.27 s) the moment it passes another, and two crossing rows never overlap text. The axis rescales smoothly as the maximum grows.
- The hero moment, about 3.4 s: the climbing entry passes the leader and takes first place. Its bar turns orange (colour tween over 0.2 s with a brief brighter flash), a small `#1` badge pops next to its name (`easeOutBack`), and the year number punches (scale 1 to 1.1 to 1).
- 4.3 to 5.0: hold on the last year (the counter lands exactly on it). Idle: a shimmer travels across the orange bar, labels stay steady.

# /zack-d-films: a 3D cartoon x-ray explainer in three.js

Example subject: "what coffee does to your brain". The user may give any cause and effect inside a body: sugar and mood, sleep and memory, a workout and muscles.

## The skill: /zack-d-films

This one is 3D. Use three.js, loaded from one pinned CDN script and nothing else:
`<script src="https://cdnjs.cloudflare.com/ajax/libs/three.js/r128/three.min.js"></script>` (global `THREE`). Use a WebGL canvas of 1080 x 1920, `preserveDrawingBuffer: true`, `antialias: true`, scaled to fit the window as described in The frame. The rules in How every reply works (pure function of `t`, loop dip, seeded randomness) apply the same way: write `render(t)` that sets every object's position, rotation, scale, opacity and light from `t`, then calls `renderer.render`.
Everything is built from code: no models, no textures, no loaders. If WebGL is not available, show one centered line of text saying so instead of a blank page.

### Look
- Style: soft 3D cartoon, like a toy. Rounded shapes made of spheres, capsules and cylinders, smooth shading (`MeshStandardMaterial`, roughness 0.55), gentle warm key light from the upper left, soft cool fill from the right, a little rim light. Rounded character proportions: a big head (about 40 percent of the figure height), small torso, short arms. Fix one thing most beginners get wrong: the chin must flow into the face. Make the head one sphere scaled slightly wider and flatter at the bottom, no separate chin piece.
- Ground: a soft pastel gradient background (mint to pale aqua, `#bfeee0` to `#e6fbf5`) set as the scene background colour or a big gradient plane. No floor, no horizon.
- Character: the subject's person, shown from the chest up, centered, facing the camera, in a bright simple sweater (mustard `#e8b52f`), skin warm and simple, hair as a rounded cap shape plus a bun or other single feature. Big round eyes with a dark pupil and a small highlight, small brows, a small mouth. She holds the subject's object at chest height (the mug for coffee).
- X-ray: the upper head is made of two layers, an opaque skin-and-hair layer and a teal glass layer (`MeshPhysicalMaterial`, transparent, opacity about 0.35, a fresnel-style bright rim). Inside sits the organ that the subject is about (a brain made of bumpy pink-coral spheres for coffee, a heart, a stomach), hidden at first. Scan reveal: a thin bright teal horizontal plane sweeps down, and behind it the opaque layer fades to glass while the organ fades and scales in (0.85 to 1, `easeOutBack`).
- Particles: 50 to 60 small glowing yellow spheres (`#ffd84a`, emissive) that travel from the object into the organ.
- Caption: a short two or three word line in weight 800 white with a dark soft shadow, set as DOM text over the canvas in the lower third (for coffee: `caffeine kicks in`). Words pop one at a time.

### Beats (seconds)
- 0.0: the figure at rest, warm light, mug at chest, steam wisps rising (a few translucent spheres drifting up). The head is fully opaque, the organ hidden. A slow camera dolly in runs the whole clip (about 4 percent, `easeInOutCubic`).
- 0.0 to 0.8: she lifts the mug toward her mouth (arm and mug rotate up), head tilts back 6 degrees, eyes close at 0.6 s. The bun (or any hair feature) bounces 0.1 s after the head move.
- 0.8 to 1.15: the sip: the mug tilts 12 degrees and back, cheeks puff 4 percent.
- 1.15 to 1.55: the mug comes back down to chest height, eyes open.
- 1.35 to 1.75: the scan sweeps down from the top of the head to the eyebrows, the skin and hair turn to teal glass, the organ appears.
- 1.55 to 4.0: the particles leave the mug one after another (0.03 s apart), each travelling for about 0.9 s along a smooth curve from the mug rim, past her cheek, into the organ (small seeded offset per particle), at a steady speed, fading in at the start and shrinking as they enter.
- 2.65 to 3.75, the hero moment: as particles arrive the organ lights up in three pulses (emissive intensity and a soft glow sprite at 2.65 s, 3.05 s and 3.45 s, each brighter). At 3.2 s her eyes pop wide (scale 1 to 1.25 with `easeOutBack`, pupils 15 percent smaller), brows lift, a small smile widens, the bun bounces again.
- 3.3 to 3.75: the caption pops in word by word (each word scale 0.6 to 1 with opacity, 0.13 s apart).
- 3.75 to 5.0: hold the look. The organ glow breathes (slow sine), a few particles still trickle, steam rises, a tiny head bob (1 degree), one blink at 4.3 s.

The guide

Six short steps. Pick the way that fits your Claude: step 1 for the Claude app, step 2 for Claude Code, step 3 if you have no skills.

  1. In the Claude app: add the skills

    Open the Claude app or claude.ai, then Settings, then Capabilities, then Skills. Turn skills on, click upload and choose a skill ZIP from this page. Upload the six ZIPs one at a time.

    Open a new chat and type a command with your subject, for example /isometric a tiny ramen bar. Claude builds the animation as an artifact next to the chat.

    Tip Upload the six single ZIPs (isometric.zip, globe.zip and so on), one at a time. The all-in-one ZIP is for Claude Code in step 2.

  2. In Claude Code: unzip into the skills folder

    Unzip motion-skills.zip into ~/.claude/skills/ so each skill sits in its own folder (for example ~/.claude/skills/isometric/SKILL.md). On Windows that folder is %USERPROFILE%\.claude\skills\. Restart Claude Code, open a session and type a command with your subject.

    Tip If a command is not found, check that SKILL.md sits directly inside the skill's folder, not one folder deeper, then restart Claude Code again.

  3. No skills on your plan? Copy as prompt

    Click Copy as prompt on a card above, open a new chat in Claude, paste, and add your subject on a new line at the end, for example Subject: a tiny bookshop. Send it.

    Tip The prompt is long on purpose, it holds the whole recipe. Paste all of it, then add your subject below it.

  4. Black screen or an error?

    Type fix the error and send it. Claude repairs the file and shows it again. If it is still broken, send the same command again in a new chat.

    Tip Animations are long files. If the reply stops halfway, type continue, or start a new chat and send the command again.

  5. Turn it into a video

    Open the animation in the artifact view and make the preview as tall as you can. Screen record it: on a Mac press Cmd+Shift+5, on Windows press Win+Alt+R, on a phone use the built-in screen recorder. Record one or two loops, then crop the video to 9:16 in your editor.

    Tip Record the animation on its own, with nothing else on screen. Each loop lasts 5 seconds, so a 10 second recording holds two.

  6. Post it

    Upload the 9:16 video as a Reel, TikTok or Short. Add a caption that names the command you typed, so people can try it too.

    Tip Post the version you like best. Run the same command a few times with different subjects and keep the strongest result.

Questions

Why does it take a minute?

Claude writes a whole animation as code, and these files are long. Expect about a minute or two for the reply, then a few seconds to play. Longer subjects with many parts can take a bit more.

Does it cost money?

The skills and this guide are free. Skills in the Claude app and in Claude Code need a paid Claude plan with skills turned on. Without skills, Copy as prompt works in a normal chat, but a long reply can get cut off on a free plan, so a paid plan is safer.

Can I use it commercially?

Yes, the animations you make are yours to post, sell and use in client work. Check Anthropic's terms for your plan for the details.

Can I change the subject?

Yes, that is the point. Type the command, then any subject in one line: /isometric a tiny bookshop, /globe every flight leaving London, /zack-d-films what sugar does to your mood.

Why does mine look different from the reel?

Every run is built fresh, so no two results match. The clips above went through one round of fixes after the first result. Expect a simpler first version and tell Claude what to change, for example "make the title bigger" or "slow the camera down".
